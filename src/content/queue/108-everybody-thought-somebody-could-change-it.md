The software that decides how much home care funding an older Australian receives recorded 2,851 cases of its own classification outcome being overridden between 1 November 2025 and 25 May 2026. No assessor or delegated decision-maker held the legal power to override that outcome. The Department of Health, Disability and Ageing has told the Commonwealth Ombudsman it is working through those cases to establish whether the overrides were lawful (Commonwealth Ombudsman, 2026).

The number appears on page 64 of an own motion investigation published on 26 October 2026, which finds, under the *Ombudsman Act 1976*, that the Department’s administration of the aged care Single Assessment System has been unreasonable. The Ombudsman went further than the question he had set himself: a reasonable person would, in his view, “lack confidence that the system is in fact fair and consistent” (Commonwealth Ombudsman, 2026, p. 11).

An assessor visits an older person and completes the Integrated Assessment Tool, a form the Department says runs from 125 to 340 questions. Two algorithms convert those inputs into an annual funding level, one of eight from $10,731 to $78,106, and a priority category that sets the wait. Once the assessment section is finalised, neither the assessor nor the delegate who approves access can edit the result. Between 9 December 2024 and 25 March 2026 the system produced more than 600,000 needs assessments.

The absence of a human override is not an accident, and the reason deserves a fair hearing. On the Secretary’s account, Government decided in 2024 that funding and priority for ongoing home support would follow assessor inputs and the algorithms, with no power for an assessor to override the result. The Act and the Rules were drafted to match. Under the old Home Care Packages program, where discretion ran further, departmental analysis put 20 to 25 per cent of approvals at Level 4 in 2024-25 against a baseline forecast of 15 per cent, reaching 50 per cent in some jurisdictions. The Department calls that a partial driver of $4 billion in unspent funds (Commonwealth Ombudsman, 2026, agency response).

Consistency of that kind is a defensible objective. What the consultation never did was put that aim to anybody as a choice. The draft Act used the phrase “computer decision-making”, and the consultation paper said such arrangements were expected for classification and priority, while other material in the same paper said those decisions would be made by a human. The public was asked no question about whether outcomes should be changeable, and the component of the draft Rules holding those provisions appeared last, two months before commencement.

Inside the Department the confusion ran further. Its own guidance told assessment providers that funding levels could move up or down. The tool was built with override functionality. Instructions to the technical and legal drafters described the classification decision as “computer-assisted” rather than fully automated. In October 2025 a senior executive wrote internally that the Department had “recently discovered a serious disconnect which means our public messaging and IT system design is out of sync with our policy authority and the ... Rules” (Commonwealth Ombudsman, 2026, p. 63). The Ombudsman’s phrasing is harder: the Department “did not, and may still not, understand key components of the legal framework it created”.

Which brings the 2,851 back into view. The tool needs that functionality so assessors can route people into short-term care pathways, and it also lets them change a funding level, which the Rules forbid. The Ombudsman is careful about what the number proves: it covers a small fraction of assessments, so the ratio describes prevalence, while the effect on each of those older people is completely unknown.

Little in the system was built to notice. Across the same window as those 600,000 assessments, the Department conducted 3,658 audits or reviews of needs assessments, 56 of them targeted. Against that volume, it is 0.6 per cent. The review path is no better placed. An internal reviewer must also use the tool, so the algorithm decides a second time; the reviewer’s extra power is to change the inputs and run it again. Asked whether the Administrative Review Tribunal may depart from using the tool on merits review, the Department did not know, and said it was considering the question.

Parliament has already voted on the remedy, twice, in opposite directions. The *Aged Care Amendment (Restoring Human Override for Aged Care Needs Assessments) Bill 2026*, moved by Senator Anne Ruston with Senators Penny Allman-Payne and David Pocock, passed the Senate on 2 July 2026 and then failed to find support in the House of Representatives on 11 August. The government’s alternative passed both chambers on 15 October and received assent on 26 October as Act No. 98 of 2026, the same day the Ombudsman’s report appeared. A Greens amendment that would have restored clinician override immediately was voted down by both major parties (Australian Greens, 2026).

The new law creates a referral, and the power to act on it stays with the Department. Under exposure draft rules released on 12 October 2026, an assessment organisation refers a case to a departmental delegate. That delegate may change a home support classification only where the person’s circumstances are not adequately reflected in the criteria for their level, and are therefore significantly different from other people at the same level. The person must also be at risk of significant harm without more care. On a priority decision the delegate may not assign the urgent category. A sunset clause secured by Senator Ruston confines the pathway to decisions made on or before 31 December 2027. Tim Hicks of Bolton Clarke said a test of difference from others at the same level allows “uncommon problems to be addressed, but it doesn’t allow situations that occur regularly, like the under-classification of clinical needs” (The Weekly Source, 2026b).

Senator Jenny McAllister put the government’s position directly on 14 October: “The secretary of the department is ... a human. His delegates are human”, the human override senators had sought. She told the chamber that a broad assessor discretion “potentially adds billions of dollars of cost into the system. That’s what the department analysis says” (Senate, 2026). The Ombudsman’s first recommendation asks for a bill letting assessment delegates substitute their own decision, and the second for the rules and guidance to apply it. Both drew a “noted” from the Department. Seven of the remaining eight were accepted in whole or in principle, and the tenth asks the national audit office to consider a performance audit. On 23 October the Ombudsman told the Secretary that his findings remained unchanged.

For anyone running a rules-based eligibility system, the lesson is where to look. [Whether the person in the room has standing to refuse an automated outcome](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/08/06/the-human-in-the-loop-needs-a-way-to-say-no/) was the subject of an article here in August 2026, and this case adds the harder half of it. Three things have to be true at once: the power to substitute is written into the legal instrument rather than the build or the training material, somebody specific holds that power, and the agency counts how often it is used. Counting at 0.6 per cent does not meet that third test. A real count would have put the distance between what the guidance promised and what the Rules allowed into an audit file long before 2,851 decisions needed checking.

The Department’s defence amounts to saying the law was right and its own advice was wrong. Take that defence at face value and the implication remains uncomfortable: assessors were taught to expect a power the Rules never gave them, and the build let them use that power anyway. The power the new pathway supplies reaches only decisions made up to the end of 2027, and the review that might produce a durable fix reports by the middle of that year. The first repair is the cheap one: write into law who can change the answer, and count the times they do.

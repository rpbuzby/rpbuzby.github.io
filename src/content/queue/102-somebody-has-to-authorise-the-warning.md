The median wildfire in a new European study covered 52 hectares when its satellite detection first became publicly visible, and 557 hectares by the time anybody published a news story about it. The figures come from a preprint posted on 22 October by Daniil Anastachas and two co-authors at the International Scientific Youth Community (2026). They pulled active-fire detections for Europe and the Mediterranean from FIRMS, the public NASA feed that distributes them, covering 10 August to 10 October this year. Clustering 273,273 of them and removing industrial heat sources along with crop burns left 345 wildfires in 23 countries, 330 of which began inside the window and could be timed.

For each fire the authors recorded when it became visible in several public channels, including the satellite feed, a Copernicus Emergency Management Service activation and verified multilingual news. That feed made a fire public after a median of 2.2 hours. A Copernicus activation followed at a median of 18.8 hours, though only 11 fires ever received one. The first news article appeared at a median of 29.7 hours, and only 77 fires, 23 per cent, were covered at all. By the time an activation came through, the median fire measured 791 hectares, larger than at first news coverage. Nine fires carry that figure against 68 for the news median, so the two are not strictly comparable.

Those numbers deserve their caveats. The paper is a preprint from a youth research community that has not been peer reviewed, covering two months of one season in one region, and a collector outage cost the authors a milestone for 62 fires. Their first interval is not a decision by anyone either: the 2.2-hour median matches NASA’s own statement that most near-real-time products are available within three hours of observation, so what it measures is orbits and processing. Their growth equation is a modest result honestly reported. On the growing steps it was fitted to, it beat an assumption of no growth only narrowly, at 8.4 per cent median error against 8.9. Once the steps where nothing grew are counted back in, the no-growth assumption wins outright. It did better on an independent test against ten Californian fires, at 26 per cent against 49.

The paper is careful about what it did not measure. “Most importantly,” the authors write, “the study measures when information was published, not when authorities knew about a fire or acted on it.” Their future work section names the study nobody has done: with the cooperation of fire services, it would be possible to measure when agencies actually learned about each fire, “which would separate the public delay from the operational delay”. So the paper says nothing about whether European agencies were slow. It establishes that an outside observer has no way of finding out: the authors timed publication to within an hour and could not time the response at all.

The European figures do not transfer here, for an instructive reason: Australia’s first link is much faster. That European interval belongs to polar-orbiting sensors and the processing behind them. DEA Hotspots, by contrast, draws on Himawari-9, a geostationary satellite that covers Australia at all times. The product is “updated with new information every 10 minutes”, and the page states that “at best, hotspots information is 17 minutes old” (Geoscience Australia, n.d.). Anyone with a browser can watch it.

The same page says what that feed is not for. “DEA Hotspots is not to be used for safety of life decisions,” it says, and hotspots “are not presented in real-time and not designed to be used in isolation of other data sources” (Geoscience Australia, n.d.). Both statements are correct and responsibly placed: a hotspot is a thermal anomaly rather than a confirmed fire, and acting on one without verification is how people drive towards trouble. The consequence is that someone in a fire-prone valley can see something on a map 17 minutes after a satellite saw it, and is told not to act on it. They must then wait for the one thing they are allowed to act on, which is a warning.

Warnings in Australia come through the Australian Warning System, with its three levels running from Advice through Watch and Act to Emergency Warning (Australian Institute for Disaster Resilience, n.d.). What happens between the hotspot and the message is set out jurisdiction by jurisdiction. Victoria’s arrangements, in force since 1 October 2025, are clear about where this sits: “the control agency holds responsibility for issuing warnings”, and warnings “should be authorised by the Incident Controller” before they go out (Emergency Management Victoria, 2025). The principle it files under Timely runs to three lines without a figure in any of them, asking that warnings are issued promptly and that each update arrives before the previous warning expires.

The arrangements are candid about the interval itself. “During the initial stages of some emergencies,” the document says, “it is possible that there may be no warnings provided to the community or that warnings may contain minimal information” (Emergency Management Victoria, 2025). That is an accurate description of early fireground reality and I would not write it differently. It also describes a period of unknown length, and I cannot find anywhere that Australia routinely publishes how long it lasts. Other numbers from a fire season get published. The time from first detection to the first Australian Warning System message does not appear in anything I can find.

Europe’s 18.8-hour median has a cause of the same kind, because a Copernicus activation has to be asked for. “Only Authorised User can request the service,” the Copernicus page states: national focal points and EU bodies, with other public authorities obliged to route a request through one of them (Copernicus Emergency Management Service, n.d.). Those 18.8 hours are a queue of people rather than a queue of photons.

All of which reads as an operating-model problem more than a technology one. SmartSat CRC, the Commonwealth-funded space research centre, made the point in March while describing a future in which satellites watch almost continuously: once that arrives, “the constraint shifts from detection latency to analytical capacity”. The body named for that work in the same piece, the Australasian Space Innovation Institute, is described as “driving the systems integration that the sector currently lacks” (SmartSat CRC, 2026). I wrote separately about [a modelled price for seeing every fire in a state](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/10/26/a-price-for-seeing-every-fire-in-a-state/), and the same gap applies there. Better sensing shortens the first interval, which here is already short, and leaves the second untouched. That one is a chain of verification and authorisation, and no procurement shortens it unless somebody has specified who owns it and what may be published before ground truth.

I have been part of that chain as a volunteer, and what I noticed was how little of it is timed. Nobody involved could tell you how long their part took. A measure would not be hard to produce: time from first detection to the first published warning, reported after the season at the incident level. Both timestamps already exist, one in the fire’s own record and one on the warning; nothing in the current reporting puts them side by side.

AFAC’s outlook for this spring, published on 19 August, flagged heightened fire risk in northern and south-eastern New South Wales, south-east Queensland and north-eastern Tasmania. It also named parts of Western Australia, South Australia and Victoria, along with large parts of the Northern Territory (AFAC, 2026). A warning issued on time still has to arrive, which is its own problem. Between 19 December 2019 and 31 January 2020, 1,390 telecommunications facilities were affected, with a median outage of 1.6 days, according to figures from the communications regulator, ACMA, reported by ABC News (2026). I wrote on the measurement end of that in [a piece on the new cell-broadcast system](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/10/19/changing-the-siren-mid-season-who-tells-a-household-which-alert-counts/), which cannot report whether a message arrived.

The European preprint found that satellite information is public roughly a day before most people hear about a fire. The Australian version would be shorter and more awkward, because here that information is public within the hour and comes with an instruction not to use it for safety of life decisions. Everything after that point is a decision made by a person with a title, and it is the only step in the sequence that nobody times.

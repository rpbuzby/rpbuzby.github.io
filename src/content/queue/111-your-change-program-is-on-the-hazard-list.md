Eight hundred and fifty-eight public servants from federal and state agencies filled in The Mandarin’s Frank and Fearless survey this year. Forty-six per cent of them reported high or very high stress at work, 44 per cent of federal respondents and 49 per cent of state ones (The Mandarin, 2026). Brenton Prosser wrote the result up on 28 October, called it “a 20% jump in 12 months, after a 5% rise the year before”, and added the caution it deserves: “This is one survey, and it should be read that way” (Prosser, 2026).

He is right about that. Two thirds of the respondents are aged 45 or over, how they were recruited is not published, and 858 people are not the Australian Public Service measuring itself. The jump is ambiguous as well, because a fifth more than last year and twenty points on top of last year are different findings and the wording does not settle which. Anyone quoting the result should stop at the direction: more respondents called themselves heavily stressed this year than last.

The survey is still worth reading, though not for the size of the rise. A result like that has had an address in Commonwealth law since 1 April 2023, and the people who most need to read it that way run change programs.

On that date Division 11 of the Work Health and Safety Regulations 2011 (Cth) came into force, inserted by the Work Health and Safety Amendment (Managing Psychosocial Risk and Other Measures) Regulations 2022. Regulation 55A defines a psychosocial hazard as one arising from or relating to “the design or management of work”, a work environment, plant, or “workplace interactions or behaviours”, which “may cause psychological harm”. Regulation 55C turns that into an obligation: a duty holder must manage psychosocial risks and implement the control measures regulation 55D requires.

Regulation 55D is specific in a way that should interest anyone running a transformation. In determining control measures, the duty holder “must have regard to all relevant matters”. Among the matters it then names are “the design of work, including job demands” and “the systems of work, including how work is managed, organised and supported”. Both phrases belong to operating model design, and they name what a restructure exists to change.

Nineteen months later the obligation acquired its manual. The Work Health and Safety (Managing Psychosocial Hazards at Work) Code of Practice 2024 was made under section 274 of the Work Health and Safety Act 2011 on 27 October 2024, and commenced the day after its registration on 1 November. It identifies 17 psychosocial hazards, among them low job control and poor organisational change management (Comcare, n.d.). An approved code is not advice an agency may take or leave. By its own foreword it is “admissible in court proceedings”, and courts “may rely on the code in determining what is reasonably practicable”.

The code’s description of poor organisational change management reads like the risk section of a program plan nobody wrote. It names “insufficient consultation” and a failure to weigh “new hazards or performance impacts when planning for, and implementing, change”. Its worked examples are blunter. One is “not considering health and safety risks when downsizing, relocating or introducing new technology”. The code is also explicit about timing: “you should consider psychosocial hazards at the design phase when planning an organisational restructure.” Not at implementation, and not in the staff survey afterwards.

Letitia Hope, deputy president of IPAA ACT, gave the mechanism in one line when the survey results were discussed in a Mandarin Live webinar. Stress, she said, “can arise when accountability exceeds influence or control” (Holmes, 2026b). That is regulation 55D in plain English. Demands up and control down is the exact shape the regulation tells a duty holder to look for, and it is what most change programs produce in the months between announcement and settled new structure.

Hold the survey’s other numbers against the code’s list and they stop looking like a wellbeing story. Seventy-one per cent of respondents said they use AI in their job, against 28 per cent in 2024, and the figure was 74 per cent among Commonwealth respondents (Holmes, 2026a). Forty per cent reported being bullied or harassed, up from 27 per cent two years ago (The Mandarin, 2026). Bullying and harassment are on that list in their own right. Rapid AI adoption is not, but the worked example of poor change management already covers introducing new technology, so the question for an agency is whether anybody assessed the rollout.

The shift is not new to emergency services, where the same move from measuring the worker’s resilience to testing whether the organisation manages the exposure produced [Australia’s first national framework for tracking firefighter trauma](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/07/06/from-resilient-individuals-to-responsible-organisations-australias-first-firefighter-trauma-framework/) in 2026. What has not travelled is the application to ordinary office change. Programs measure adoption and the volume of change landing on a given team, which is [the number agencies already collect and do not use](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/09/17/easy-to-count-how-change-volume-became-the-only-number/). A psychosocial risk assessment puts a question neither of those does: what the change does to demands and control while it is under way, and who is exposed for how long.

The service does have a better instrument than a pulse survey, and it has reported. The 2026 APS Employee Census ran from 4 May to 5 June with about 150,000 participants across 113 agencies. In October the Australian Public Service Commission published the service-wide highlights, earlier than the end-November date its spokesperson had given the Canberra Times (Athwal, 2026). Most indexes came in a point below 2025, including the senior executive manager one and the wellbeing measure. Neither movement settles anything, and the steeper falls reported in September rested on APS averages from two very small agencies that the commission could not validate. The fuller account arrives with the State of the Service report.

Census results will not answer the question the Act asks anyway. Section 27 requires an officer to exercise due diligence to ensure the organisation complies with its duties, and the Act spells out what that means. An officer has to keep up-to-date knowledge of work health and safety matters and understand the hazards of the operation. They must ensure there are processes to minimise those risks and to act on hazard information promptly. Then they must verify that it happens. An officer is anyone who makes or participates in making decisions affecting the whole or a substantial part of the business. That is a functional test rather than a job title, and in a department it reaches the people who sit on the committee that approves a restructure. Since the Act defines health as “physical and psychological health”, the duty they must be diligent about already covers psychological harm.

In practice the document that discharges it is short. It names the hazards the change introduces and says who is exposed to them and for how long. It sets out each control measure with an owner, and records what the affected people said when they were consulted. Last, it fixes a date to check whether the controls worked. The code asks for that record because it is how a duty holder demonstrates compliance, and supplies a risk register at Appendix C.

None of this requires an agency to promise anybody an unstressful job, and the regulations do not treat stress as a failure; the obligation is narrower and more awkward than that. A duty holder has to predict what a redesign will do to the people inside it, write that prediction down, and act on it while the structure is still on paper. Any survey arrives too late to help with that, however well it is run.

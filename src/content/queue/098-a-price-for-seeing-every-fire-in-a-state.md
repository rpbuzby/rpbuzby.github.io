Four researchers have put a price on seeing every fire in a state, and it is smaller than the capability conversation around fire detection would lead you to expect. In a preprint posted to arXiv on 16 September this year, Romain Puech and three co-authors optimised two things agencies normally buy separately: where detection infrastructure sits, and how a fleet of autonomous drones moves between those sites. Tested out of sample against 3,693 Californian ignitions recorded from 2021 to 2024, their modelled network, sized to a budget of US$100 million over five years, detected 97.3 per cent of those fires. Within the first hour it had found 74 per cent of them (Puech et al., 2026). No such network has been built. These are simulated results for a hypothetical deployment measured against historical ignition points, in a paper that has not yet been peer reviewed.

At that budget the optimiser bought 200 charging stations and 1,400 drones, and no ground sensors whatsoever. They only enter the solution once the budget is five times larger, because at current technology costs the drones are the better buy. Amortised across five years, that budget is about US$20 million a year, which the authors put at roughly 5 per cent of California’s annual wildfire-prevention expenditure. Australian fire detection tends to get discussed as a frontier that arrives when the technology matures and the business case clears. Expressed as a share of a prevention budget that already exists, it becomes an allocation decision, and those are much harder to defer.

I wrote separately, in [Sub-Minute Detection, Multi-Year Adoption](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/05/19/sub-minute-detection-multi-year-adoption-the-wildfire-procurement-gap/), about the distance between what detection technology can already do and how long agencies take to adopt it. That argument has always had a hole in it, which is that nobody could say what detection at population scale would cost, so the capability conversation never had to become a budget one. It does now, at least for one state, and the question changes shape once a figure of any kind exists. An agency that says no to a detection network is no longer refusing an unpriced frontier technology. It is turning down something with a published price somewhere, and the first thing it will be asked is what its own version of that price would be.

Two findings further down the abstract matter more to a buyer than the headline percentage. Detection, the authors report, is governed primarily by spatial coverage, while routing strategy mainly determines detection speed. Read as a purchasing instruction, that splits one product into two. Coverage raises the share of ignitions you see at all. Routing cuts the time until you see them. The two have different cost curves and separate acceptance tests, and a specification asking for “detection capability” as one requirement lets a vendor build whichever half is cheaper and still pass.

The modelled parameters show how far apart those purchases sit. Coverage, in this paper, is 200 docks sited on burnable land, each holding up to seven drones with about an hour of flying before an automatic battery swap. Each of those drones is credited with certain detection out to 2,900 metres, a radius the authors call deliberately conservative. Speed is what the routing solver does with that fleet across the risk map. Buy the first and leave the second to a vendor’s own logic and you get a network that sees nearly everything eventually. At this budget every detected fire but one was found inside four hours, and anything still unfound after six counted as missed. Eventually is a different product from within the hour, and the case for spending on detection at all rests on the second.

The most useful part of the paper is what it says it has not costed. Its figures are five-year hardware total cost of ownership: US$100,000 a ground sensor, US$150,000 a charging station, US$50,000 a drone. The authors state that these exclude airspace authorisation and staffing costs, and they note that continuous beyond-visual-line-of-sight patrol at this scale is not authorised in the United States today. The optimisation also restricts placement to burnable land and leaves ownership and power access for later work.

For an Australian reader those exclusions are the decision. Flying beyond visual line of sight here is not simply something an agency buys. It requires an operator holding a remotely piloted aircraft operator’s certificate, and a remote pilot licence with a pass in the instrument rating or the beyond-visual-line-of-sight exam, or supervision by somebody who has one. The flying itself then needs a BVLOS approval assessed under the regulator’s AusSORA framework, and CASA’s own guidance says approvals may take several months from the date of payment (Civil Aviation Safety Authority, n.d.). Scale that to continuous patrol from hundreds of sites and the hardware is the costed half of a detection network, while permission and the people who hold it have no price attached.

So no Australian figure follows from this study, and quoting one would be the error. The optimisation ran on two inputs: a historical ignition record from the United States Forest Service, and a burn-probability surface calibrated on ignition rates from 2006 to 2020, with every test year held out of it entirely. Whether Australia holds equivalents of both at that resolution is the first question here, and it sits ahead of any budget. The national bushfire picture has a history of its own, which I wrote about separately in [A Governance Problem Dressed as a Data Problem](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/07/07/a-governance-problem-dressed-as-a-data-problem-australias-national-bushfire-picture-expires-this-month/). An optimisation is cheap to run. The record it runs on has to be collected and kept current by somebody, and that work has nothing to do with drones.

What a specification can do with all this is split the requirement and test each half on its own terms. Coverage becomes the share of recorded ignitions falling inside a detection footprint, measured against an ignition record the buyer supplies and the vendor does not get to choose. Speed becomes time from ignition to first detection, measured against that same record. Authorisation and crewing get costed as their own line items before any hardware is priced, because neither has a published unit cost and the approval alone runs to months. The paper recommends none of that. It is an optimisation study and says nothing about tenders. The split is simply what its two mechanisms imply for whoever has to write one.

Victoria’s Fire Danger Period began at 1am on 5 October across two Gippsland shires, which CFA’s chief officer put two weeks ahead of the previous season’s start, in conditions he described as exceptionally dry (Country Fire Authority, 2026). A network of the kind this paper models would change what an agency knows in the first hour of a fire like that. It would not change how many crews are available at three in the morning, which was the subject of a separate piece on [counting crews for a third night](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/10/23/ask-how-many-crews-can-work-a-third-night/). Detection has a published price somewhere in the world now, which leaves the approvals and the crewing as the unpriced end of the problem, and neither of those gets cheaper with better optics.

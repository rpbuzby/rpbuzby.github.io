In 2023 and 2024, fewer than 600 United States federal employees took more than 90 workdays of paid administrative leave. In 2025, almost 100,000 of them did.

That comparison comes from the US Government Accountability Office, which went looking in September for what the federal workforce reduction had cost (GAO, 2026). The mechanism was the deferred resignation program. The Office of Personnel Management told agencies to reassign or eliminate the duties of anyone who accepted it. Those employees then went onto paid administrative leave until they resigned or retired, generally by 30 September 2025. People stopped working and kept being paid. That was the design, and none of it was hidden.

GAO estimates that the agencies in its review spent around US$9.5 billion in salary costs on paid administrative leave in 2025, a sixfold increase on 2023, with about US$6.7 billion of that associated with the deferred resignation program. About 144,312 employees recorded leave for that purpose. Workdays of such leave across government went from about 4 million in 2023 to about 21.6 million in 2025, peaking in July at nearly 3 million. The review covered 76 agencies holding about 95 per cent of the civilian workforce, so this is close to the whole picture.

None of that, on its own, settles whether the program was a bad deal. OPM’s leadership expects it to save more than US$20 billion a year, and a one-off cost against a recurring saving can be perfectly good arithmetic. GAO does not claim otherwise. Its finding is smaller and worse: OPM does not know the actual cost. Leave used for workforce reduction went into the same category as every other kind, so it cannot be separated out afterwards. As the report puts it, to calculate long-term savings OPM needs to know short-term costs, and it cannot do that with any accuracy.

The failure is worth pausing on, because it is so ordinary. GAO found agencies logged 144 per cent more paid administrative leave in pay periods containing a public holiday. Holidays are not supposed to be coded that way at all; a separate category exists for them, and agencies picked the wrong one often enough to show up as a statistical artefact. OPM does not plan to correct those historical errors in data it has already released. Nobody falsified anything. A chart of accounts was asked to track something it was never built for, and it produced numbers that look authoritative and cannot bear weight.

So GAO made a recommendation that sounds almost clerical: create a new category in the payroll system, so leave used for workforce reduction is reported separately from everything else. OPM agreed to create it.

Hold that answer, because a request of the same kind got a different reception here.

Australia has been running the opposite transition for four years: converting work bought from consultants and labour hire firms into public service positions. Budget Paper No. 4 for 2026–27 (Commonwealth of Australia, 2026) records 13,200 roles created through external labour conversions, about a third of all growth in public service staffing since 2022, with nearly 1,400 more in 2026–27 including 1,250 frontline roles at Services Australia. The instrument is the Strategic Commissioning Framework, under which agencies set annual targets for bringing core work back in house. I have written before about [the capability case for doing this](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/07/27/months-to-destroy-years-to-rebuild/), which I think is strong, and I say so as someone who sells consulting to the Commonwealth and is therefore arguing against part of my own market.

The Auditor-General examined the scheme and reported on 28 September, a fortnight after the American report landed, concluding that it has been partly effective. The Australian Public Service Commission was largely effective in helping agencies implement it, and the service-wide reduction target for 2024–25 was exceeded.

Then the findings turn. The audit concluded that the design limited the scheme’s scope to a narrow part of external workforce spending, and that entities could meet their targets while overall spending on the external workforce increased (ANAO, 2026). It found that public reporting improved transparency over implementation and target achievement, but did not show whether reliance on the external workforce for core work had fallen or public service capability had strengthened. Most pointedly, the targets as designed were not an effective way to measure whether the scheme was achieving its intent.

The numbers show how that happens. In 2024–25 agencies reported bringing more than $542.3 million of core work back in house, against a combined target the audit puts at $527.6 million. A target exceeded, reported as a win. Canberra Times coverage records that Defence and the Australian Taxation Office accounted for $388.4 million of that, while close to two thirds of participating agencies fell short of their own goals. In the audit’s 22-entity sample, total supplier expenses rose from $32.7 billion to $35.6 billion in one year (Athwal, 2026b). The framework covers less than 3 per cent of the roughly $20 billion the Commonwealth spends on an external workforce annually.

Now the sequence that should trouble anyone who believes in the policy. For 2025–26 the combined target dropped to $438.9 million, and 59 per cent of agencies set no target at all, on the basis that they outsource little or no core work. A year earlier that figure was 27 per cent. Over the same period, AusTender figures reported by the Canberra Times put spending on temporary personnel contracts at $3.53 billion, up from $2.33 billion (Athwal, 2026a). Those two figures count different populations and neither explains the other, but they move in opposite directions while the program reports success. The Commission then sought and received approval to streamline reporting for 2026–27, which removed the targets altogether.

And when the Auditor-General recommended that government strengthen and standardise public reporting of supplier expenses, including those for the external workforce, the Department of Finance noted it. Not agreed: noted, on the basis that financial disclosure remains a matter for individual agency heads. The Commission agreed to the recommendation aimed at it, on aligning the scheme with other requirements. The reporting one, which would let somebody eventually answer the question, went unaccepted.

The American auditor asked for a new reporting category and got a yes. The Australian auditor asked for better disclosure of the same spending and was told it rests with each agency head. The weaker answer came from the government running what I take to be the better program.

This is narrower than the familiar complaint that the wrong thing is being counted, which I have [made at length elsewhere](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/09/17/easy-to-count-how-change-volume-became-the-only-number/). In both countries the transition itself, the churn of moving work between workforces, was booked as an adjustment to business as usual rather than as an undertaking with its own cost and result. The American transition produced billions in real payments that cannot be attributed. The Australian transition produced a target that was met, then diluted, then deleted, with no finding on whether capability improved.

Which leaves the policy undefended. If in-housing works, its supporters should want the separate accounting more than anyone, because a program that cannot be shown to have worked will not survive a government that would rather it had not. What the budget can show is inputs: 13,200 converted roles, and a savings figure bundled with reductions in non-wage spending such as travel and property, which cannot be decomposed into the part that came from external labour. What it cannot show is whether the in-house capability was built. Four years in, we have made something substantial and still cannot say what it achieved.

Recommendations about data categories are the dullest instrument in public administration, and usually the only reason anybody later finds out what a decision cost. Ours was left to agency heads in the same month the targets were removed, which means the next review of Australian in-housing will have less to work with than this one did.

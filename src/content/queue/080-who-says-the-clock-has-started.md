On 23 August, Michael Pezzullo opened an essay on The Strategist with a planning assumption rather than a threat. “Before 2020, Australian defence strategy was predicated on a planning assumption that we would have at least 10 years’ warning of an impending war” (Pezzullo, 2026). He is a former deputy secretary of defence and led the Home Affairs department until November 2023, so he is describing machinery he used to run.

The assumption went in July 2020, and the wording deserves to be quoted exactly. “Previous Defence planning has assumed a ten-year strategic warning time for a major conventional attack against Australia. This is no longer an appropriate basis for defence planning” (Department of Defence, 2020). The same document added that Australia “can no longer rely on a timely warning ahead of conflict occurring”.

That was six years ago. The 2023 Defence Strategic Review and the 2024 National Defence Strategy restated it. The 2026 National Defence Strategy says it again, inside what Mick Ryan (2026) summarised on release as “more ambition, same architecture”. Four documents across two governments have now told us the decade is gone. What I cannot find in any of them is the schedule that was re-baselined when it went.

The 2026 strategy is attached to real money. The government’s April announcement put about A$425 billion over the decade, with an additional A$14 billion over four years and A$53 billion over ten. Defence funding is projected to reach roughly 3.0 per cent of GDP by 2033-34 (L. Cohen, 2026). Cohen’s qualifier is the one to hold onto, because that percentage is the NATO measure, which includes military superannuation, defence housing, veterans’ benefits and parts of the intelligence agencies. It is not the appropriation. On his arithmetic the NATO measure would need between A$111.0 billion and A$129.5 billion in that year, while the headline appropriation plans A$106.7 billion.

A strategy that withdraws its own lead-time assumption has to show what changed underneath the assumption, and the place to look is whatever has the longest lead time of all. In the Integrated Investment Program the slowest items to arrive are guided weapons and air and missile defence, both bought mostly from one overseas supplier.

Guided weapons did well out of the 2026 program on any reading. Approved investment rose to more than A$6 billion from A$820 million in the 2024 program, and planned ten-year spending went from between A$16 billion and A$21 billion to between A$26 billion and A$36 billion (Jones, 2026). Then comes the sentence that gives Madi Jones’s piece its title: “None of this can be certain, however, because the government has put all GWEO spending into just one line in the IIP.” Her reading of the dependency underneath it, published a week after the program was released, is blunter still. “Australia remains almost wholly dependent on the United States for guided weapons”, she writes, with American delivery times on key munitions “stretching into years rather than months”.

Air and missile defence received between A$7.2 billion and A$10 billion over the decade for active capabilities across all three domains. The allocation also covers counter-drone systems for deployed forces and a medium-range ground-based system to sit above the NASAMS batteries. Malcolm Davis named the risk the day after the program was released, writing that “wars have drained the West’s stock of interceptors” (Davis, 2026). His recommendation was that government prefer suppliers who support full local manufacturing.

Four months later the risk stopped being a forecast. On 4 August, CNN reported that the US military had exhausted nearly 80 per cent of its THAAD interceptors, with senior commanders describing the stockpile as “dangerously low” (Z. Cohen et al., 2026). Secretary of Defense Hegseth publicly disputed the reporting the following day. Hold the claim as contested; the estimates around it are not tidy either. CSIS put Patriot inventories “now under 1,000” and THAAD “at about 250” in late July (Cancian & Park, 2026b). David Axe, writing in The Strategist on 20 August, put the remainder much lower still at potentially 70 THAAD rounds and 870 Patriot. He hedges the figure twice himself (Axe, 2026).

The restocking timetable is what matters most to a prospective Australian buyer. Patriot missiles requested in the FY2027 budget are projected to begin delivery in May 2029, and THAAD interceptors in mid-2029 (Cancian & Park, 2026a). Axe puts build times at 42 months for a Patriot round and 53 for a THAAD. Lockheed Martin is increasing THAAD production from 96 interceptors a year toward some 400, under a contract that runs seven years (Mulrine Grobe, 2026). The queue Australia would join is not empty either: half of PAC-3 MSE deliveries already go to allies and partners, who have bought nearly 1,900 of that variant since 2020 (Cancian & Park, 2026a).

Put those figures beside each other. Australia has committed up to A$10 billion over ten years to a category its sole supplier cannot restock before 2029, inside a strategy whose first premise is that the decade is gone.

The domestic answer is real but it belongs to a different category. Lockheed Martin has assembly operations in South Australia for GMLRS rounds, Kongsberg is moving toward domestic manufacture of JSM and NSM cruise strike missiles, and rocket motor facilities are progressing to the 2024 plan (Jones, 2026). Every one of those is strike; none is an interceptor, which is where the shortage sits.

Other countries read the same evidence and reached a different conclusion. Ukraine’s Fire Point is building an interceptor called the FP-7.x inside a nine-country European coalition, with an infrared seeker sought from Germany’s Diehl Defence and command-centre integration by Norway’s Kongsberg. The proposed unit cost is US$700,000 against roughly US$4 million for a PAC-3 (Axe, 2026), though Fire Point’s own published target has run closer to a million. It has not yet intercepted a ballistic missile; Fire Point puts that test by July 2027. A proposed price for an unproven missile buys Australia nothing today. What it shows is that the shortage has changed what other countries believe they are permitted to build for themselves.

The habit is not uniquely Australian. On 20 August the US Government Accountability Office reported on the Army’s Next Generation Command and Control stack. The Army means to field it across 11 divisions and four corps by the end of US fiscal year 2032, with costs so far documented at about US$3.3 billion. GAO found that the Army “has not developed a detailed schedule that identifies the specific units, by fiscal year, that will receive the modernized capabilities”. Nor has it “completed an initial cost estimate for the life cycle of the collective effort” (GAO, 2026a). The next day GAO reported that the Department of Defense had set a goal of funding 90 per cent of its installation maintenance needs while the services fund about 80 per cent. The deferred backlog has risen from US$137 billion in FY2020 to US$285 billion in FY2025 (GAO, 2026b).

Each of those has the same shape: a stated goal, a spend figure, and a line underneath that does not reach the goal. Australia’s version is the one nobody has priced, because it sits at the top of the document instead of inside a program. A schedule is a set of assumptions about lead time. Withdraw the lead-time assumption and every schedule beneath it still contains the old one, having simply stopped being written down anywhere a reader can check it. That is why the single GWEO line matters more than its size. One undifferentiated line lets nobody outside Defence test whether the sequencing changed when the assumption did, and that is the thing a withdrawn warning time ought to change. I made a version of this argument about Osborne earlier this year; this is the same question with a delivery date attached.

Pezzullo’s essay takes the missing decade as settled and asks what replaces it. His answer runs to warning machinery that works and political leadership willing to act on what intelligence tells it, set inside a security assessment wider than Defence. He writes about the Office of National Intelligence under its Director-General Kathy Klugman, and he points back to the 2024 Independent Intelligence Review. That is the right question and a harder one than money, because money can be announced in April and machinery has to be staffed and exercised.

Warning time never promised ten years of grace. The number was an estimate of how long Australia would have between noticing and needing to be ready. Deleting the estimate did not shorten the noticing or speed up the getting ready. It moved the whole weight onto whoever is responsible for saying the clock has started, and six years on, that job still has nobody’s name on it.

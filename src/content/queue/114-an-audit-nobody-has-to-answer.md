Investigators at the United States Government Accountability Office spent part of this year finding out whether a commercial AI platform would help somebody plan a terrorist attack. They settled on two scenarios after talking to outside experts. One was an improvised explosive device designed to cause mass casualties. The other was an attack on the electricity grid. They took the four most-used generative AI platforms as at April 2026. The report names all four: ChatGPT and Gemini alongside Claude and Grok. Each was assigned at random to one of the two scenarios, and the testing ran in the northern spring. The safeguards worked at first. Platforms refused requests and stripped information out of answers. In some cases they suspended the account. The investigators got past all of it using what the report calls “common safeguard-bypassing techniques”, and each of the four produced a viable plan in under twelve hours (Government Accountability Office, 2026a).

Then GAO went outside and checked whether the plans would work. Investigators took photographs and conducted surveillance. They consulted external experts to check what the platforms had told them, then procured transportation and materials to confirm their availability. What the platforms produced was specific. It named targets and locations, and in some cases the timing of an attack. The report, released publicly on 18 November, is careful about what any of this proves. None of the underlying information was new, and the finding concerns assembly speed instead. The platforms, GAO writes, “were able to synthesize it into viable plans within hours”.

GAO is equally careful about the limits of its method, which it sets out while answering one of the companies. Anthropic argued that a proper evaluation of dangerous capability needs a control and a sample large enough to reach statistical significance. GAO accepted that and printed it: “We acknowledge that our tests reflect a point in time and are not a statistically significant sample.” It also accepted that because the platforms were put against their scenarios at random, the four results cannot be compared with one another. The claim being made is a narrow one.

The surprise in the report is the shape of the document built around that finding. This was a full performance audit, conducted from December 2025 to October 2026 under generally accepted government auditing standards. GAO sent a draft to the four companies for review, exactly as it would to an audited agency. All four wrote back. Their letters appear as appendices, and the report works through them one by one, conceding some points while rejecting others. Every stage of the audit process ran to completion. At the end, in the place where a GAO report puts its recommendations, there is nothing at all.

The report never explains that absence, so the reading of it here is mine. There was nobody to address. GAO audits United States federal agencies, and none of them built or operated the thing that failed. Kevin Walsh, who directs GAO’s information technology and cybersecurity work, described the underlying difficulty to Nextgov: “These products are not fully in the control of the companies that are operating them” (Dille, 2026). An auditor can describe a safeguard that failed inside a product sold by a private firm. Directing somebody to fix it is a different power, and GAO does not have it here.

Which makes the Australian version of the question worth asking. Had that report been written here, who would the recommendation have gone to?

The National AI Plan answers, although not in those words. Published on 2 December 2025, it sets the government’s approach as building on “Australia’s robust existing legal and regulatory frameworks”, with established laws as the foundation. Agencies and regulators, it says, “retain responsibility” for potential AI-related harms “within their respective policy and regulatory domains” (Department of Industry, Science and Resources [DISR], 2025). The same chapter adds that “every organisation developing and using AI is responsible for identifying and responding to AI harms and upholding best practice”. The recommendation goes to the buyer.

So look at what the buyer is required to do. An Australian agency running a commercial AI platform across its staff sits under the Policy for the responsible use of AI in government, now at version 2.1, which applies to all non-corporate Commonwealth entities with some exceptions. The policy requires an impact assessment for every in-scope use case (Digital Transformation Agency, 2026). That assessment must begin at the design stage and be finalised before the solution is deployed, with any agreed risk treatments applied. A high-risk case goes to the agency’s accountable official and to a governing board or senior executive. Once the agency decides to deploy it, the case goes to the DTA as well, and is reviewed at least every twelve months. These are demanding obligations, and the argument here is not that they are thin.

The testing requirements sit in a companion document, the technical standard for government’s use of AI, and its coverage is not in doubt. The standard applies “regardless of whether an agency develops an AI system in-house or contracts an external provider to build or supply it”. Its own list of what falls in scope includes “publicly available AI tools, such as ChatGPT”. Each criterion within the standard carries a marking. Required criteria are the ones agencies must satisfy. Recommended criteria are those they should.

Statement 28 of that standard is the one about testing. Under the heading “Agencies must”, it requires safety measures to be tested through methods including fault injection, and specifically “testing for incorrect or harmful inputs”. Under “Agencies should”, it asks for adversarial testing, “attempting to break security and privacy measures to identify weaknesses” (Digital Transformation Agency, 2025). The test GAO ran is the second sort, and in Australia that is the optional one. The examples the standard offers for that criterion sit further away again. They run from poisoning a training set to reverse engineering a model, and what the criterion aims at is security and privacy measures. GAO went after something else: the refusal a hosted product gives a user it should be turning down.

Suppose an agency decided to run the test anyway. GAO’s other report that week, published on 20 November, names the obstacle: “there are no widely accepted evaluation criteria for assessing offensive or defensive capabilities of generative AI” (Government Accountability Office, 2026b). That report offers sixteen policy options to United States policymakers, one of which would “restrict autonomous and nondeterministic AI in consequential functions pending certification”. No certification scheme exists to wait for. An agency wanting to test a vendor’s safeguard would be inventing both the method and its scale at once.

Australia’s AI Safety Institute is the body closest to that work, and its description of itself repays a careful read. The institute analyses and tests new AI models. It supports regulators and agencies responding to emerging risks. It works with the Australian Signals Directorate and CSIRO, and contributes to international joint testing of frontier AI systems. With the labs that build them it collaborates “under memorandums of understanding” (DISR, 2026). The verbs on that page are cooperative ones. Nothing there lets the institute require a developer to submit a model or compel a change to one. The plan asked for a body that informs the people holding the risk, and that is what was built.

The gap at agency level is smaller than an AI act and more fixable. I have written separately that the mandated AI use case register [does not record which contract brought a system in](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/11/09/ask-the-register-then-ask-the-contract/), which leaves nobody able to audit that register against what the agency bought. The impact assessment has a version of the same gap. An assessment captures the risk and the treatment. It does not ask who tested the treatment.

One question is worth putting to your own paperwork before Christmas. Find the impact assessment for the general-purpose AI tool most of your staff use. Find the line where a built-in safeguard is doing the work of a mitigation. Then ask who tested that safeguard and against what criteria. For a hosted commercial platform the answer is the supplier, working to its own schedule and its own criteria. That belongs in the assessment, in those words.

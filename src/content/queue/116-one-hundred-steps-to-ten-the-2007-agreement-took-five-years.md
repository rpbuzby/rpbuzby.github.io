On 15 November, at a cruise terminal on Manila South Harbor, the Philippine Secretary of National Defense and Australia’s ambassador to the Philippines signed a new Defence Cooperation Arrangement (Baroña, 2026). Anthony Albanese watched the signing, in Manila for the ASEAN summit (Reuters, 2026). Gilberto Teodoro Jr, who signed for the Philippines, then told reporters what the arrangement would do for his department. “Rather than 100 steps to do a single thing, it will just now take 10 and it will be more purposive and not ad hoc.” The steps he means are the procedures for mounting a joint activity, and he supplied a label for the arrangement as well: “It is the anti-red tape measure of our SOVFA” (Lee-Brago, 2026).

That is an unusual promise for a defence announcement. The stated benefit is administrative and it comes with a figure attached. Teodoro put it more concretely still in other remarks the same day: “At least there will no longer be a need to secure multiple permits. Everything will be streamlined into a single process so our Australian allies can respond immediately during emergencies” (Baroña, 2026). Permits and one-off memoranda go in, a single process comes out.

The strategic reading of all this has been well made. Jennifer Parker argued two days after the signing that it amounts to “a recognition that Australia’s defence does not begin at its shorelines”, on top of being a vital update to the relationship (Parker, 2026). She is right, and it is not what Teodoro was describing. His claim is about procedure and it has a number in it, which deserves the reading practitioners give any approvals redesign.

The Australian description is flatter. The joint release from Albanese and his defence and foreign ministers says the arrangement “provides a contemporary framework to guide priority areas of cooperation”, expanding on an existing memorandum of understanding. The release names seven of those areas, running from enhanced maritime awareness through to defence infrastructure development (Prime Minister of Australia, 2026). Richard Marles speaks of deepening practical cooperation and creating new opportunities. The step count does not appear. Nor does the Status of Visiting Forces Agreement, the instrument Teodoro says the new arrangement exists to make work.

That agreement is worth knowing about, because it was already in place and it is what everything else hangs beneath. Defence engagement between the two countries ran for twelve years on a memorandum of understanding on cooperative defence activities, in effect from 22 August 1995. Parliament’s treaties committee described that memorandum without ceremony: it “does not make provision for the status of forces in a host country and is not legally binding in international law” (Joint Standing Committee on Treaties, 2007, para. 3.2). The treaty that answered both objections was signed on 31 May 2007 by Brendan Nelson and Hermogenes Ebdane Jr, during a visit to Australia by President Gloria Macapagal-Arroyo.

The reason it was needed at all sat on the Philippine side. The committee recorded that a treaty was essential for the Philippines to broaden its defence engagement. Its constitution “requires a treaties-status agreement before allowing Australia to undertake more extensive defence activities in Philippines territory” (Joint Standing Committee on Treaties, 2007, para. 3.5). The treaty clears a constitutional threshold. By itself it authorises nothing: it “will not authorise either country to deploy troops or conduct operations in the other’s territory”, and does no more than fix the status of forces once the two governments arrange to send and receive them (para. 3.4). Training areas and the use of facilities were left to be mutually determined afterwards. Teodoro’s description of the November document is therefore exact. “So basically, it is the mechanism by which the SOVFA will work” (Maitra, 2026).

The same parliamentary file records what happened after the signing. That treaty was signed in May 2007 and did not take effect for five years. Under its own article 28 it entered into force only once both governments notified each other in writing that all their procedures were complete (Joint Standing Committee on Treaties, 2007, para. 3.15). The Philippine Senate did not concur until 24 July 2012, at which point the Australian embassy in Manila welcomed the ratification and noted that it “now paves the way for its entry into force” (Australian Embassy Manila, 2012). The agreement was catalogued that year as [2012] ATS 31, five years after two defence ministers put their names to it.

Negotiation was long over by then. Those five years measured the distance between a signature and any practical effect, and every month of it sat inside one country’s domestic machinery. What filled those years was each government’s own procedure for bringing the text into force, and one of those procedures needed all five.

Australia’s own contribution to the step count in 2007 is on the public record too, and it is not short. The committee noted that the wording had been agreed by the Executive Council on 23 May 2007, that “all agencies cleared the text”, and that it was then “endorsed by all relevant Ministers” (Joint Standing Committee on Treaties, 2007, para. 3.17). The committee had to recommend “that binding treaty action be taken” (para. 3.19). No new primary legislation was required, although by-laws under the Customs Tariff Act 1995 needed amending (para. 3.16). Not one of those steps is written in the agreement. They belong to the machinery around it.

This is the ordinary condition of a process claim that spans two organisations, and the usual place such claims come undone. An instrument can standardise the join between two systems, replacing several permits with one application and a memorandum per activity with a standing framework. It cannot reach inside either organisation’s own queue, which the document nowhere describes. Elapsed time on the Australian side of a short-notice combined activity sits with Australian approval authorities and the clearances behind them, and the November document reaches none of that.

Which leaves the question of whose hundred steps were counted. Teodoro’s figure describes his own department’s experience of running activities under the visiting forces agreement, and he is the person best placed to know it. No Australian figure was published alongside it. That is an observation about what was released on the day, not a finding about Australian practice. A ministerial release had no reason to quantify anything, and a department may well hold a number it has never printed.

The arrangement took time to produce as well. It was signed about fifteen months after Teodoro and Marles signed a statement of intent on enhanced defence cooperation on 22 August 2025 (Lee-Brago, 2026). An instrument whose whole purpose is to compress procedure took that long to agree, which is nobody’s failing: negotiation is slow because the internal steps are real.

Australia draws these boundaries on paper and then discovers where the steps actually sit. On 9 November the country’s first National Armaments Director took office, with a published accountability that starts at Second Pass approval and so leaves [someone else to confirm the requirement](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/11/11/who-confirms-the-requirement/). Deciding the need and owning what comes after are two different jobs. Who owns the count of the steps is a further question again, and in Manila it was answered on only one side.

So the test ahead is narrow and it arrives soon. Take the first short-notice combined activity run under the new arrangement, and measure the days from request to approval on each side against the last one done the old way. That measurement will tell anybody who needs to know whether ten steps describes the whole process or one country’s part in it.

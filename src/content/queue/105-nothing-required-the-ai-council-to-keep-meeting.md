The United States Department of Defense has a body called the Responsible AI Working Council. Its chief digital and AI office set it up as the collaborative group where the people who have to apply the policy compare notes. On 14 October 2026 the Government Accountability Office published an audit recording that the council did not meet from late 2024 until August 2025 (GAO, 2026). No system misfired in a way that reached a newspaper. The council stopped convening, and the mechanism that eventually noticed was an auditor with a questionnaire.

The gap was not invisible to the people inside it. GAO surveyed ten departmental components about ten selected AI use cases. Officials at four of them said they had run into responsible AI implementation problems during the period the council was not meeting, the kind collaboration would have helped with. Six of the ten said that sharing resources and good practice would benefit their work. Nothing obliged the council to meet, though, and nothing required anyone to report that it had not.

The council resumed in August 2025. GAO’s concern is that nothing prevents it stopping again, because it has no charter defining roles and responsibilities or specifying how often it meets. Its two recommendations both run to the Secretary of Defense, who is asked to have the Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Officer issue a charter for the council and attend to the workforce needs of the Responsible AI Division. As of February 2026 three of its ten authorised positions were filled. The chief digital and AI office itself, GAO notes, “has experienced staffing shortages that have hindered its ability to take these actions”. The department concurred with both. GAO also records that its earlier recommendations on AI collaboration and the AI workforce have not been fully addressed, and suggests Congress consider directing the department to implement them.

It would be easy to read this as a story about an organisation that never got around to AI governance, and that reading would be wrong. The department established AI ethical principles in guidance and defined six tenets to guide responsible AI implementation. It also issued an AI strategy in 2026 that emphasises speed in AI development. So the written architecture exists, and most of the ten components GAO surveyed reported taking at least initial steps to establish oversight and implement those tenets in the use cases examined. What failed is the part no framework describes, this one included: whatever obliges a coordinating body to keep meeting once the principles are written.

Australia’s closest equivalent document is Defence’s Policy Settings for Responsible Use of Artificial Intelligence in Defence, publicly released in March 2026 (Department of Defence, 2026). On its central question it is better than most civilian AI policy. Responsible use, it says, requires an identifiable Defence official to be specifically accountable for the decisions and outcomes of AI-enabled functions. “Accountability cannot be transferred to a technology.” The accountable officer changes as a capability moves through its life cycle, and that transfer has to be documented. Delegating authority down the chain of command does not move the accountability anywhere.

Read all eleven pages and one thing is not there. The document names no standing internal body responsible for coordinating implementation. It records that Defence has established a Defence Artificial Intelligence Centre through which it “governs, manages and accelerates” its use of AI. The policy calls that body a central hub of expertise, with functions that include workforce professionalisation and the development of mechanisms to monitor and assure compliance with government and Defence policies. A centre is not a meeting. No membership is specified and no frequency is set. No staffing figure appears anywhere in the document, nor a budget, nor a review date. The oversight the policy does describe is external and periodic: the inspector-general who watches the intelligence agencies, the Independent National Security Legislation Monitor, three parliamentary committees, and the Auditor-General reporting to Parliament.

That absence is not evidence of a failure and should not be dressed up as one. Defence has no obligation to publish its internal governance instruments, and a working group that meets every month would not necessarily appear in an eleven-page public policy. The document says Defence will meet its obligations “by adjusting and strengthening existing governance and controls, or establishing new mechanisms as needed”. The narrower point is what the public record can answer. Nothing published says which body coordinates this work, or how often it sits. That is the question GAO put to the Department of Defense. Nobody has publicly put it here.

Lesley Seebeck’s essay of 13 October 2026 puts a harder version of the same problem (Seebeck, 2026). An accountable officer, she writes, “is accountable for a capability”. Nobody answers for the frame: for what the system put in front of the decision-maker and what it left out, and for how urgent it made the choice look. “Explainability is a matter of post hoc audit,” she adds, “but mediation happens at the speed of the decision.” She also places Defence at “the system’s centre of mass” for government AI use, and argues that the norms Defence sets will not stay inside Defence. If she is right, the continuity of Defence’s own coordination stops being a departmental housekeeping matter.

There is one forum where the question could be put now. The Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence was appointed by resolutions of both houses on 20 August 2026 and must present its final report by no later than 30 November 2026 (Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence, 2026). Submissions closed on 14 September 2026 and 571 were received. The terms of reference name defence once, as “defence industry”, among sectors of Australian comparative advantage. The department itself falls inside a different term, the one covering adoption of AI by Commonwealth departments and agencies and “the transparency and accountability measures that apply to AI use”, which was written with service delivery in mind. A committee holding 571 submissions against a fixed reporting date is unlikely to go looking for the meeting cadence of a Defence coordinating body, and nothing in its terms points it there.

I have written before about [the mismatch between how widely AI runs through the Defence investment program and how little governs it](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/06/29/funded-across-the-budget-governed-by-one-policy-the-adfs-ai-integration-gap/). This is a different failure from that one, and a smaller one. Failures this size are the harder kind to see: a policy can be well drafted, a principle can be correct, an accountable officer can be properly appointed, and the forum meant to join that work up can stop meeting without a single document in the set becoming untrue.

The fix GAO recommends is unglamorous and cheap. Write the charter. Put the membership and the meeting frequency in it. Fill the positions that were authorised. Then name the person whose job it is to notice when the meetings stop. The alternative is what the Department of Defense got: an outside auditor asking, more than a year after the meetings resumed, whether anything had gone wrong in the meantime. Even that auditor had to be sent. A Senate committee report on a defence authorisation bill included a provision for the review, which is a long way to go to find out whether a working group still meets.

Public sector plans are good at naming work and dates. They are much weaker at naming the person who will stop doing something else so the new work can happen. A plan reaches its approval meeting with its commitments and timeframes attached, usually with a budget line beside them. The question of whose existing workload gets rearranged is settled later, by whoever inherits the list. That sequence looks like administrative tidying. It is closer to the thing that decides whether the plan happens.

One Commonwealth office does go back and ask whether an agency did what it said it would. In September The Mandarin reported the figures from the Commonwealth and ACT Ombudsman’s fourth insights report in that series. It tracked 54 recommendations from 12 investigations finalised before the end of October 2024, of which 78 per cent were fully or partially implemented by the end of 2025 (Holmes, 2026). Four times in five is a creditable rate for any organisation, and the Ombudsman, Iain Anderson, is entitled to report it.

Underneath the headline the figure splits. Twenty-five of the 54 recommendations were fully implemented, which is fewer than half. Seventeen were partial. Two were not implemented, and ten were recorded as superseded, close to one in five. What that last category covers is defined in the report itself, which I could not open, so these figures come from The Mandarin’s account of it. Even read generously, the result says that where an external office has asked a specific question and written down the answer, agencies move. It says nothing about the volume of work nobody outside is tracking.

The same day, The Mandarin ran an opinion piece by Sarah Nesbitt, an independent consultant who spent two decades in the Victorian public sector. Her question is the one agencies seldom write down: when there is too much work, who decides what gets done? The diagnosis will be familiar to anyone who has sat through a planning session where the work was agreed and the capacity was not. Demand for policy advice and service delivery outruns what an agency has available, and the pressure is worse in a savings climate with a growing agenda and no additional resources. The gap between the delivery burden and the capacity to meet it keeps widening (Nesbitt, 2026).

Set the two beside each other and they describe one system from opposite ends. Agencies follow through at a decent rate on the commitments somebody external is still asking about. Everything else goes to a rationing decision that happens informally, every fortnight, in hundreds of conversations, and is recorded nowhere.

That decision has a name in the implementation literature. Christoph Knill, Yves Steinebach and Dionys Zink call it policy triage. Their argument is that implementation research has studied individual policies, while organisations are actually coping with an accumulating stock of them. Obligations pile up faster than the administrative resources to meet them, so they get rationed. How much rationing an organisation does depends on how vulnerable it is to overload and on whatever compensating capacity it can find (Knill et al., 2024). The evidence is European, tested through four comparative case studies of environmental policy implementation in Ireland and England, so the mechanism travels better than the specifics.

Vulnerability is the term that matters here. Triage of this kind is a rational response to an impossible list, and what survives the triage is the work somebody outside the organisation can see. The Ombudsman’s implementation figures describe the same mechanism from the other side: attention is the scarce input, and an external office with standing to ask is what supplies the attention.

Which returns me to the approval meeting, where the cheapest version of this is available. Aggregate slack is the wrong thing to ask about in that meeting: few agencies could calculate it honestly, and the figure would be set aside if one did. What can be asked there are three questions about each commitment in the plan. Who specifically does this work. What are they doing now that stops. Who decided that. In most of the plans I have read in federal and state agencies, that mapping does not exist in any form, and the people who would have to produce it were not in the room.

The reason it does not exist is structural. A plan with an explicit stop-doing list has to name losers before it has been approved. It tells a branch head that their program is the one that stops, in a document that will be read by the executive, the minister’s office and eventually a committee. Ambition in a plan is free at the point of approval and subtraction is not, so the plan that clears the room is the one that promises everything and sequences nothing. The capacity question then gets asked months later, when it is no longer a question about the plan but a question about individuals who are already behind.

The sequence that follows has been traced in detail. Yutong Chen, writing in the Australian Journal of Public Administration, tracks the JobSeeker Payment reform from 2014 to 2025 and names the pattern: asymmetric decoupling, where “political authorisation is withdrawn before the underlying problem has been resolved” (Chen, 2026). The pandemic expansion worked in the short term and produced no durable institutional change, because the window closed before the problem did. Read that alongside a plan never costed against capacity and the order of events is predictable. The shortfall shows up as delay, which is then read as a delivery failure rather than a planning one, and the political sponsor moves on well before the delivery does.

The obvious objection is that somebody in the organisation surely raises it. The evidence on how change actually moves through rule-bound institutions suggests otherwise. Tanja Elina Havstorm and colleagues at Örebro University interviewed agile coaches working in Nordic public organisations and found a consistent method. It is gradual adaptation: practices adjusted to the structure and culture of each organisation and to the rules it works under, through dialogue and the slow accumulation of trust. Centralised decision-making and risk-averse cultures were among the challenges they named, and the response was gradual change inside the existing structures instead of a direct challenge to the rules (Havstorm et al., 2026). The practitioners best placed to see the capacity gap are, on this evidence, the ones whose method depends on not turning it into an argument.

The measurement half of this already has its own article: the measure exists, because Australia asks its public servants whether change is managed well in their agency, and [nobody sequences anything off the answer](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/09/17/easy-to-count-how-change-volume-became-the-only-number/). That piece was about the measure. This one is about the earlier moment when the measure would be used, and about a number far easier to produce than a census: one name against one commitment.

The uncomfortable part belongs to my own industry. Consultants are the supply side of document-centred planning. We are paid to produce the plan, judged on the plan as a document, and the capacity calculation that would make the plan smaller runs against our commercial interest. I have often been asked to make a plan more ambitious. I have never once been asked to test it against the delivery capacity of the client’s own staff.

Adding that test costs nothing. It is a column beside each commitment, holding the name of the person who does the work and the thing they put down, and it can be filled in while the plan is being approved. On the Ombudsman’s own numbers, though, the pressure to fill it in will come from outside the agency, if it comes at all.

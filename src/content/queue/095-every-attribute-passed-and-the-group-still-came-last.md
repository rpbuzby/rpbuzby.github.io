Eighteen large language models were each handed the same 32 applicant profiles ten times over and asked to rate them for consumer credit, then for a job, then for a rental property. The applicants’ stated finances and work histories stayed constant, and the only variables were demographic: gender, race, age, citizenship, education. That is 17,280 ratings. The group that finished lowest, averaged across every model, was White men without undergraduate degrees.

Read one attribute at a time, the same data looks like a system behaving itself. Women were rated above men in all three settings. Black applicants were rated above White ones, and degree holders above those with none. Each is the direction a fairness check hopes to see. The group at the floor shows up only once the three attributes are read together, which almost no bias-testing regime does.

The study is Maxim Chupilkin’s, from Oxford’s Department of Politics and International Relations, submitted to arXiv on 17 September 2026 and not peer reviewed (Chupilkin, 2026). It is a full factorial design: 32 fictional profiles, rated ten times by each of 18 models from 12 developer groups, on a zero-to-100 scale. Averaged over the models and over age and citizenship, White men without degrees scored 75.87 in credit and 92.71 in hiring, with 86.62 in rental housing. Black women with degrees were highest in all three settings. White men without degrees held the lowest or second-lowest mean in 46 of 54 model-scenario combinations.

Credit shows a gap of 2.94 points and hiring 3.66, with rental at 3.56. Confidence intervals at 95 per cent clear zero and reach about five points at most. On scales where the bottom group already averages in the high seventies to the low nineties, three points is small. The result deserves attention for its consistency instead: the same group at or near the floor across models from a dozen separate organisations.

Chupilkin is careful about the limits of the design, and anyone citing it should be too. The design’s profiles are fictional and no live decision was made, so whether differences of this kind affect real access to credit or housing “depends on how models are used in actual decisions”. The design is American, with its loan and rent amounts in US dollars, and its citizenship variable runs only between Western and non-Western. Race in the design is a binary of Black and White, with nothing for Indigenous identity or the culturally and linguistically diverse categories Australian agencies report against. None of that makes the design wrong, and all of it argues for replicating it here before claiming it transfers.

What makes the finding awkward is how closely it fits what Australian bias testing already asks for. The Commonwealth’s technical standard for government use of AI gives Statement 6 to managing system bias, in three criteria introduced by “Agencies must” (DTA, 2025a). The statement names the protected attributes it has in mind, from “age, disability, race” through to gender identity and sexual orientation. It warns that an aggregate number “could hide underperformance on a minority population”, and the fix it offers is to break the number apart: “Disaggregating performance metrics into suitable attributes can detect whether a system performs fairly”.

That is sound instruction and it would catch a great deal, yet it says nothing about attributes in combination. I searched the whole text of Statement 6 for intersection and intersectionality and then for combination and subgroup, and none appears in it.

The convention has a respectable origin: discrimination law enumerates grounds one by one, and an assurance clause written to mirror the law inherits the enumeration. The difficulty is arithmetic, because attributes combine multiplicatively while a test suite grows one line at a time.

My own version of this is blunter than the research will support, so I will mark it as mine. A test suite assembled from a list of expected victims will only ever find expected victims. That is an argument about insufficiency and not about futility: single-attribute testing catches single-attribute harm, which is the kind agencies have been found committing before. Keep every one of those tests. Passing every test is no longer evidence of a fair system, because a model with the profile Chupilkin describes would pass the lot.

Bias testing also assumes one system at a time, and that assumption is harder to repair, because the failure is organisational. Agencies assure systems and deploy them in sequence. Evdoxia Taka and Michele Sevegnani of Glasgow, with Temitope Lawal of Northumbria, put the problem into the proceedings of a multimodal interaction conference in Naples, published on 5 October 2026. They drew on 25 stakeholder interviews across the criminal justice system of England and Wales. They found outputs of AI used for transcription and classification being fed into downstream tools (Taka et al., 2026). Their argument is that “errors and biases introduced at early stages may be propagated” downstream, where they can also be amplified or transformed. The people most exposed are those whose speech and communication “diverge from normative assumptions embedded in training and evaluation data”, a category the authors extend to accent and to disability-related patterns and distress. Their example is transcription: even minor inaccuracies “may distort evidential narratives and affect legal outcomes.” They ask for chain-level assurance that makes provenance and uncertainty visible to practitioners, and shows where errors propagate. It is a position paper and measures no propagation rates.

A separate preprint puts a number on the handoff itself. Zhang and Wu varied only the explanation passed between two models in a question-answering pipeline while holding the evidence and the candidate answer fixed (Zhang & Wu, 2026). Faithful explanations added almost nothing to accuracy. Corrupted explanations moved the second model’s support judgement by 10 to 22 per cent, and by 34 to 55 when it was told to check what it had been handed. Blind auditors rejected or doubted nine of the ten corrupted explanations the model accepted.

Inside one system the problem is familiar, and I spent an article arguing for examining [the whole run rather than its separate steps](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/10/07/who-is-rostered-to-read-the-trace/). Between systems it changes character, because the interface has an ownership problem: assurance for system A belongs to A’s business owner and assurance for B to B’s, so the handoff can end up assured by neither. The Australian instruments make the unit of assessment concrete. From 30 September 2026, NSW agencies must use the AI Assessment Framework Platform for all new AI use case registrations and assessments, and the framework applies under Department of Customer Service Circular DCS-2026-02 (Digital NSW, 2026). The Commonwealth’s mandatory requirements include internal use case registers (DTA, 2025b). Both count use cases. In Statement 6 and in the standard’s two integration statements, which address a single system and its dependencies, I could not find a provision assigning assurance ownership to an interface between two AI systems. There are 42 statements and I read three, so take that as a reasonable search.

Neither assumption gets repaired by more testing. Exhaustive intersectional coverage is combinatorially out of reach for any test budget, and chaining multiplies it. That leaves administrative design. Choose combinations for testing by exposure, meaning the ones your decision touches at volume, and record which went untested and why. Bound what a system may decide before a person makes the determination, which turns on [whether they have the authority to refuse](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/08/06/the-human-in-the-loop-needs-a-way-to-say-no/). And keep provenance good enough to reconstruct the chain after a contested outcome, which is what a tribunal or an ombudsman will ask for.

The last of those needs a document almost no agency holds: a register showing which AI systems feed which others, with a named owner for the assurance of whatever passes between them. I have never been handed one.

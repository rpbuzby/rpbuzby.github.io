During the Black Summer fires, households on one state border could not work out whether a warning reaching them described a new fire or the one they were already watching. The states either side of them labelled fires by different conventions, so a single emergency arrived under two names, and the people deciding whether to leave had to resolve the contradiction themselves. Naderpajouh et al. (2026) use that example to argue that Australia urgently needs a national bushfire database.

Their proposal avoids the obvious mistake of a central warehouse that swallows state holdings. They describe a federated arrangement in which agencies keep ownership of their own data and the national view is assembled through interoperability. The components are metadata rules for what gets recorded about each dataset, a controlled vocabulary of standardised terms and definitions, and knowledge graphs to join records that were never built to fit together. Their account of the obstacles is unusually frank for this genre. It names funding, ongoing maintenance, unclear oversight, the problem that a responder may not know which data exists or who maintains it, and the fear among agencies that a strict national standard would limit local decisions. They add that communities can identify at-risk landscapes and vulnerable people who appear in no formal dataset, and that Indigenous cultural burning knowledge belongs in whatever gets built.

The design is sound. The difficulty is that the controlled vocabulary they propose already exists, and has been used.

In 2022 the Australasian Fire and Emergency Service Authorities Council published a *Fire history data dictionary*, AFAC Publication No. 3094, under a Creative Commons licence that permits anyone to reuse it (AFAC, 2022). Its stated purpose is national data standards for fire history, to promote consistency across Australia and New Zealand. It came out of a project on aggregating and harmonising burnt extent fire history at national scale, supported by the Australian Research Data Commons and the Minderoo Foundation. The October authors link to the Australian and New Zealand metadata standard without naming it; the vocabulary they describe as still needed has a publication number.

It was also implemented. In March 2023 Geoscience Australia issued *Historical Bushfire Boundaries* Version 1.0, covering fire history from 1900 to June 2022 (Ryu et al., 2023). The contributors were the ACT and all six states, seven agencies in total. It was mapped into the schema agreed by the participating state agencies and AFAC, which the record calls the nationally approved and agreed data dictionary for fire history. Geoscience Australia had to modify some attributes to make them fit. That is the federated model, delivered three and a half years ago. It did not stall there. A second version followed in November 2023 and a third in November 2024, each adding a season to the coverage and each built on the same agreed schema (Geoscience Australia, 2024).

So the sticking point was never agreement on a vocabulary. What became of the result is in the dataset’s own description. The Northern Territory is absent. NAFI data was intentionally omitted, the record says, because the steering committee judged it not fit for purpose on the grounds that it was not supplied by the government agency responsible for authoritative historic bushfire data. That is a ruling about the standing of the supplier, not a finding about the quality of the data. A dataset was excluded for coming from the wrong kind of institution.

Which matters, because the omitted service already produces a national annual fire report. The North Australia and Rangelands Fire Information service (NAFI) is run from Charles Darwin University, and by May 2026 had released the first of what is now an annual Fire Year Summary (Charles Darwin University, 2026). Its burnt area mapping covers 80 per cent of Australia, capturing around 97 per cent of national fire areas. The 2025 edition found the second largest fire year of the past decade, with 29.6 million hectares in the Northern Territory, 19 million in Western Australia and 14.3 million in Queensland. Dr Rohan Fisher, who manages the Territory mapping, calls it among the most accurate regularly updated data at national scale.

Those two facts sit awkwardly together. The country has a national fire dataset built to an agreed national standard that excludes the Territory, and a national annual fire report covering most of the continent that the national dataset would not accept. Both are described by their custodians as national. Neither is complete.

NAFI’s 97 per cent is a measure of area, and area is what the north has. Tens of millions of hectares burnt across the rangelands in the year that report covers. Most of that is grass and savanna, and much of it is burnt deliberately. In the south-east, extent is not what does the damage: houses and lives are lost, and forest burns at a severity that takes decades to recover from. A figure that captures almost all of the nation’s burnt hectares can still miss almost all of what the country is worried about. The south-eastern dataset can be the more useful of the two while covering a fraction of the ground. Neither service is overstating anything. They answer different questions, and nothing obliges either to do the other’s work.

The Territory has been treated as future work throughout. The 2023 version says the Northern Territory Government is progressing the development of its bushfire boundary capabilities and that work is underway to incorporate Territory-approved data in the future. The 2024 version repeats the promise, with the timing upgraded to the near future. By the third edition, apart from minor additions to the middle of 2025, there were no plans for another major update. The vocabulary held and the mapping worked. Three editions shipped, and the gap stayed exactly where it was.

The governance question sits there, and the October piece asks a different one. AFAC was also the partner on a project to build the framework for sharing bushfire data and tools between jurisdictional agencies, with $130,000 of Australian Research Data Commons co-investment, running from September 2021 to November 2023 (ARDC, n.d.). Its scope ran from what the data must contain to the governance arrangements themselves. The project page marks it complete, and says the framework will be available soon.

Compare Europe. The Forest Fire Information System was established by the European Commission with national fire administrations. An expert group has supported it since 1998, and it became a Copernicus service component in 2015. Its fire database holds nearly two million individual fire records contributed by 22 countries (European Commission, n.d.). Twenty-two sovereign states got their holdings into one place, a harder problem than eight Australian jurisdictions, and they did it by giving the arrangement a permanent institutional home. The United States has taken the same approach with NERIS, which launched in late 2024 and is rolling out in phases as the older national reporting system is retired (USFA, 2024).

I have written before about [the missing authority to compel a common schema](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/07/07/a-governance-problem-dressed-as-a-data-problem-australias-national-bushfire-picture-expires-this-month/), and about [what weak data foundations do to the models built on them](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/08/04/fewer-than-one-in-a-hundred/). This is the same failure at a later stage.

Australia did the hard part. It convened the agencies and argued the definitions out into a published dictionary. Then it built a national dataset on top of it, and funded every step as a project with an end date. What no project could produce is a custodian: an institution whose job it is to hold the vocabulary after the grant closes, admit a new supplier when one appears, and answer for the gap while the Territory is missing. That job has never been given to anyone, which is why a proposal to build a controlled vocabulary can appear in 2026, four years after one was published for anybody to use. A standard nobody is required to maintain leaves little trace in the places people go looking, and the work gets commissioned a second time.

On Thursday 29 October, a Senate estimates hearing turned to the department’s health contract with Bupa, and Defence’s Chief of Personnel answered with a figure about readiness. Lieutenant General Natasha Fox told the committee, in answer to independent Senator David Pocock, that “over 75 per cent of the ADF are actually in a deployable, military employment classification”. Reporting that day read the same figure from its other end: almost one in four Australian service members are not medically able to deploy (Greene, 2026).

The phrase matters more than the percentage. A medical employment classification is a personnel instrument rather than a diagnosis. Every member holds one, it is set against the requirements of their own military occupation, and it is reviewed as circumstances change. Two of the scheme’s five categories are deployable: fully employable and deployable, and employable and deployable with restrictions. The second of those covers members who can be sent with defined limits on what they may do, or who need health support at a specified level. The other categories cover rehabilitation, employment transition and separation. So “over 75 per cent in a deployable classification” is a weaker statement than three-quarters of the force being fit for anything, and the evidence given at the hearing does not split the two apart.

Fox was careful with the rest of it. “We have some that are temporarily downgraded,” she said, “and those temporarily downgraded in a military employment classification might be for an injury that has an ongoing rehabilitation requirement, but it’s only a temporary downgrade.” About 13 per cent, she said, were on light duties while recovering from temporary injuries, and just over one per cent were on a transition pathway out of uniform. That 13 per cent is reported against “that cohort”, with nothing to say which cohort is meant, so a reader cannot tell whether those members sit inside the quarter or beside it. It is a small ambiguity standing in for a larger one.

The recruiting news this year has been genuinely good. In September the Government announced the largest ADF in 25 years: more than 61,000 permanent members as at 1 July 2026, with more than 900 Gap Year participants and almost 1,500 full-time reservists taking the headcount past 64,000. Enlistments exceeded 8,100, the highest intake in 16 years. The separation rate fell to 7.4 per cent from 7.9, its lowest in 15 years, and Defence says it remains on track for the target of 69,000 permanent average funded strength by the early 2030s. After several years of missed targets, these are precisely the numbers a workforce program exists to move.

They are also all one kind of number. Intake and separations count arrivals and departures, and strength against a funded line records who is being paid for. None of those numbers counts availability. The number that does, the share of the force that can be committed to something, entered the public record because a senator wanted to talk about a health contract.

That is how it arrived the last time, too. In February 2011 the Vice Chief of the Defence Force, Lieutenant General David Hurley, told a Senate estimates committee that non-deployable numbers had gone from 8.3 per cent in 2009 to 9.7 per cent. Liberal Senator Guy Barnett had put it to Hurley that those numbers appeared to be worsening. Hurley declined to pin the rise on obesity, and his explanation has aged well: “We’ve got a lot of people coming back from operations who may have moved from a fit state while they are away to an unfit state” (Leslie, 2011).

Fifteen years separate those two answers, and the obvious move is to set 9.7 per cent against a quarter and call it a collapse. That comparison cannot be made honestly, because the two figures count different things. The 2011 figure counted members not deployable on medical grounds; the 2026 figure counts members holding a deployable classification, and its remainder includes people already on their way out. Neither figure belongs to a published series, so nobody reading the public record can say whether a quarter is unusual for a force of this size and age profile, let alone whether it is getting worse.

The word doing the work in Fox’s evidence is “temporary”, and it has an administrative meaning. The rehabilitation category covers conditions judged temporary, where there is a reasonable expectation the member returns to a deployable status after recovery. It runs for a defined period: up to twelve months, or up to twenty-four where a review board extends it. Temporary is a measurable claim. What preparedness turns on is the share of those periods that end in a return to a deployable classification: how many resolve inside twelve months, and how many finish in transition instead. An estimates answer gives a stock figure, while force posture is planned on the movement.

This is not abstract for anyone who has watched a plan get built. Commitments are made against units, and establishment strength is what appears on the slide. Available strength is what turns up, and the gap between them is made of medical classifications that the slide does not show.

That movement is not owned by a chain of command alone. The services that return a downgraded member to a deployable classification, from on-base primary care through to occupational rehabilitation, are delivered under contract. The ADF health services contract was awarded to Bupa in January 2019 at a value of $3.4 billion, with Serco supplying clinical staff in ADF health centres under an arrangement now extended to 30 June 2027 (Serco, 2026). The Australian National Audit Office examined how Defence manages that contract and found the department partly effective: governance arrangements largely fit for purpose, implementation of the contracted requirements partly effective (Australian National Audit Office, 2023).

The contract expires on 30 June 2027, and its replacement is being bought now. Defence has begun procurement for what it calls the Australian Defence Contracted Health System, telling the market it wants a provider able to meet the health needs of a workforce that is growing. Pathology has already gone separately to Healius in an $85 million contract, one stage of the same process (Department of Defence, 2026). Whatever the new contract counts is what Defence will be able to report once it starts, and what it leaves out will be missing from force posture work for as long as it runs. Assurance of this kind usually gets read as a question about the supplier. The buyer’s half of it, what the Commonwealth asked to be told and how often, is where the gaps tend to be, [as the other half of the KPMG review showed in a different setting](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/10/29/two-ways-to-find-out-the-half-of-the-kpmg-review-that-is-about-the-buyer/).

The encouraging part is that the measure does not need inventing. The audit office’s review of rehabilitation services under the Military Rehabilitation and Compensation Act 2004 records Defence proposing one. The rate would count how many members who finished a rehabilitation program with a return-to-work outcome had returned to a deployable medical employment classification a defined period afterwards (Australian National Audit Office, n.d.). Return to work and return to deployable are different events, and only the second restores capability. A figure separating them, published by service each year, would tell a reader more about Australian preparedness than any recruiting announcement.

Fox did not have to volunteer that figure, and she was right to. The replacement contract being bought now is where Defence could make it routine, published alongside the recruiting numbers instead of waiting on a question.

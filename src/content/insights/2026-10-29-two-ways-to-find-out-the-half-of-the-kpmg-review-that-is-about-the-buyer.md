On 29 May 2026 KPMG told the Department of Finance that the matter the two had been corresponding about since the Senate allegations of 24 March was a reportable significant event under clause 16.2.3 of its panel agreement. Three days before that assessment arrived, Finance had written to the firm’s then chief executive raising the possibility of suspension from the panel (Watt, 2026). A contractual clause whose purpose is to make a supplier tell its buyer about serious trouble produced its notification more than two months after the allegations became public, and only once the buyer had named the consequence of staying quiet.

That sequence sits inside Dr Ian Watt’s independent review of KPMG Australia, which Finance commissioned in June and published on 22 October. The findings about the firm were widely reported: serious ethical failings by some senior members, and a lengthy mismanagement of the whistleblower’s claims and concerns. Watt recorded that the firm had been warned before about weaknesses in its ethical culture and its speak-up confidence, “but did not deal with them with the rigour and urgency required” (Watt, 2026). KPMG accepted the findings and recommendations (ABC News, 2026), and the pause on its bidding for Commonwealth work now runs to 28 February 2027 (Department of Finance, 2026b).

Watt also asked what his findings implied for the Commonwealth. “There are currently no assurance mechanisms in place within the Commonwealth Procurement Framework to provide confidence that all potential significant notifications or non-compliance with the Code are being identified, and effectively managed,” the report states. Instead, without greater coordination across the Commonwealth, there is a significant risk that “some unethical suppliers will continue to win Commonwealth contracts” (Watt, 2026).

The evidence is administrative. Since the Supplier Code of Conduct took effect on 1 July 2024, the same question has been raised about other firms: MA Services Group’s compliance with the Code in late 2025, and Delivery Centric Technology Services in August 2026. Finance held no lead contractual relationship in either case, so other entities managed the response. Hence the finding that it is “currently not possible to provide a comprehensive report of suppliers who have been found to be non-compliant with the Code” (Watt, 2026). Nobody in the Commonwealth can produce the list.

That is not for want of recent work. Between May 2023 and July 2024 Finance issued procurement guidance on ethical conduct and past performance, introduced Notification of Significant Events clauses, and then developed the Code, which the procurement rules have required in all Commonwealth forms of contract (with limited exceptions) since 2024 (Watt, 2026). The Code was not in force when the conduct at issue occurred, and Watt’s assessment is that had it been, two of its expectations would have been breached.

What the build did not include was a way of finding out. Watt describes two channels through which the Commonwealth learns that a supplier may have breached the Code, and no third: identification “typically arise from notifications from the supplier (due to their contractual requirements), or media reporting” (Watt, 2026). Both ran here, and the second ran harder. By late May, Finance’s concerns had deepened for three reasons, and the first was that it “continued to learn of further allegations through media reporting and parliamentary material” rather than from timely disclosure by the firm (Watt, 2026).

Procurement complaint pathways exist, and a reader might assume they cover this. A supplier or member of the public can ask the procuring entity to review a complaint, seek internal review, take a covered procurement to the Federal Court, go to the Procurement Coordinator inside Finance, or approach the Commonwealth Ombudsman. They are “focused on whether a procurement was undertaken in a manner consistent with the CPRs, and do not address issues of whether a supplier is in breach of other policy or legal requirements, such as the Code” (Watt, 2026). The machinery examines how the buyer bought. None examines how the seller behaves.

Someone outside a firm who wants to report unethical conduct has no obvious destination either. The review found no consistent approach and no clear guidance on where a disclosure should go, so people take it to the more visible bodies, the National Anti-Corruption Commission or the Ombudsman, neither of which holds the contract. Some entities are poorly placed to assess such a disclosure anyway, and those cases need an independent assessment mechanism nobody has built.

Finance had a lever in this instance for a reason that will not generalise. It administers the Management Advisory Services panel, whose head agreement carries both the notification obligation and the Code, and through which more than half of all Commonwealth consultancy work by value was engaged in 2025-26. Clause 29.1 let Finance commission the review, and the prospect of suspension secured the agreement not to bid (Watt, 2026). The report puts the limit plainly: “The Commonwealth still cannot currently prevent a supplier from bidding for Commonwealth contracts solely because of past unethical behaviour.”

The report makes eight recommendations. Six are administrative, and Finance accepted those directed at it. Two propose changes to the framework itself, which would apply to every supplier. Recommendation 6 asks Finance to strengthen the Code framework with “strengthened sanctions, including the ability to restrict or prohibit suppliers from bidding for Commonwealth Government contracts where appropriate”, clearer reporting pathways and better information sharing between departments. Recommendation 8 asks for closer senior oversight of entity procurement.

Finance notes recommendation 6(a) to 6(f) and agrees to 6(g), the housekeeping item about treating existing contracts consistently. “The Government will consider recommendation 6(a) to 6(f) and respond in due course,” the response says, and the same formula covers recommendation 8 (Department of Finance, 2026a). Everything that would change the Commonwealth’s ability to see or sanction supplier misconduct beyond this one firm sits inside that phrase, which The Canberra Times (2026) reported as the story.

This is the second time this year the same question has been put off. In June the government responded to the Senate committee report on the Ban Unethical Contractors Bill, where the Greens had recommended a centralised debarment and suspension regime with a public register of excluded suppliers. The government noted the recommendation, describing the existing framework as providing “robust and effective mechanisms to address supplier underperformance and unethical behaviour”, and pointing to probity and integrity training delivered to 925 procurement staff. That same response named the Watt review and undertook to consider “their broader application to the Commonwealth procurement framework” (Australian Government, 2026).

The framework is devolved, with more than 80,000 procurement decisions made each year by officials across more than 100 entities. None of them can hold the view across agencies that the reviewer found missing.

When the freeze was first imposed I [wrote that the test](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/07/09/two-freezes-in-three-years-the-kpmg-suspension-and-the-accountability-that-waits-for-a-scandal/) was whether the oversight machinery would be rebuilt so the next case was caught by a control and not by a whistleblower. The report Finance commissioned about its own supplier has now answered that test: there is no such control, and the recommendation to build one is awaiting consideration.

For firms that sell to the Commonwealth, the consequence arrives ahead of the policy. Recommendation 5, which Finance did accept, gives it a basis for reinstating restrictions on this firm if it is found non-compliant with the Code on an unrelated matter. A firm that can show how a concern raised internally last year was investigated and closed is in a different position from one that asserts its culture is sound. For agencies, the work the recommendations describe is unglamorous: a single destination for third-party disclosures, and an assessor for entities too conflicted to judge their own suppliers.

The implementation plan is dated 15 November, the first quarterly report 29 January, the pause 28 February. The two recommendations that would outlast the firm have no date at all, and until the Government responds to them the Commonwealth’s answer to how it knows a supplier has behaved badly stays what it was: the supplier tells it, or it reads it in the paper.

On 9 November 2026, Kellie Parker became Australia’s first National Armaments Director. The organisation she was appointed to lead does not exist. It will be established on 1 July 2027, so for the next eight months her title is National Armaments Director Designate. What she actually runs is a transitional body inside Defence, taken over from the interim director Nadine Williams (Department of Defence Ministers, 2026).

Parker comes from mining. Until recently she was Rio Tinto’s Chief Executive for Australia, and the ministerial release announcing her appointment on 27 October credits her with more than 20 years of “complex asset development and major transformation” across iron ore and aluminium. Pat Conroy, the Minister for Defence Industry, said the appointment was about “getting better bang for buck”. Appointing an outsider drew comment at once, and behind it sits a question: whether commercial discipline works inside Defence’s accountability settings.

Defence has already published an answer to a narrower version of that question. The agency Parker will run was announced on 1 December 2025 (Australian National Audit Office, 2026). It merges Defence’s acquisition and sustainment group with those handling guided weapons and naval shipbuilding. During the transition year those functions sit inside the department as the Defence Delivery Group. On 1 July 2027 they move out. Under the Public Service Act 1999 the Defence Delivery Agency becomes an Executive Agency, and under the PGPA Act 2013, the statute that governs how public money is handled, it becomes a non-corporate Commonwealth entity (Department of Defence, 2026b, p. 11). Neither step needs a new Act, which is why this reform has reached the brink of a new agency without a bill.

The design is set out in a document Defence published in July 2026, after a whole-of-government Defence Reform Taskforce was established in December 2025 to do the detailed design work. It reinstates a formal Gate Zero, an early consideration point that tests whether a proposal should go into detailed development at all, informed by the Director’s advice on delivery options and feasibility. It adds a third source of cost contestability, alongside Defence and the Department of Finance. And it commits a financial principle to writing: “the entity accountable for a project controls the funding, while other entities access funding through agreed drawdown arrangements. Where that accountability transfers, funding transfers” (Department of Defence, 2026b, p. 23). Defence also concedes there that the costs of capability projects are often underestimated, a long-standing problem the Kinnaird and Mortimer reviews both identified.

Then it draws the boundary. “Responsibility for the delivery of military capability will move from the Secretary and Chief of the Defence Force to the National Armaments Director”, the report says. She will be accountable for “the acquisition and delivery of military capability projects that have received Second Pass approval by government”, together with sustainment and disposal. She will also provide “independent advice across the capability lifecycle on delivery feasibility” (Department of Defence, 2026b, p. 8). Second Pass is the later of government’s two approval points, the decision that fixes what will be bought and at what price. Everything before it, including the decision to pursue the project at all, happens outside her accountability.

Who holds that earlier ground is also published. The report’s map of accountabilities puts the defining of strategic direction and capability requirements with the Secretary and the Chief of the Defence Force, delegated to the Vice Chief as Chair of the Capability Investment Board. The Director advises at that stage. Her own entries on the map are to “recommend and confirm delivery baseline (cost, schedule and scope) and associated risks”, and to deliver approved capability to the agreed baseline (Department of Defence, 2026b, p. 15). A project reaches the new body with its purpose settled and its approval already given, and the new body’s accountability begins at the moment the room stops asking whether to do it.

The discussion about Parker’s background keeps missing this. Defence’s own profile of her sets out what she has done before. In the role preceding Rio Tinto Australia, it says, she “held end-to-end accountability for an integrated value chain” spanning mines and smelters with about 9,000 employees and contractors. There she “delivered a sustaining capital program exceeding $2 billion to address major hazards” (Department of Defence, 2026a). End-to-end accountability is what makes stage-gated capital discipline work, because the executive who will have to build the mine is in the room, with a vote, when the decision to fund it is made. The new office is built the other way round, as the delivery end of a system whose front end belongs to the people who set the requirement.

What the design gives her is a voice with a defined place to speak. The Director has “a unique and explicit accountability to provide independent advice” to both the Minister for Defence and the Minister for Defence Industry. The design names where that advice is owed: decisions on the Integrated Investment Program and its updates, plus the two internal gates that come before government’s approvals (Department of Defence, 2026b, p. 17). The Director co-signs the ministerial submission with the Secretary or the Chief of the Defence Force. She will also control the funding for what she is accountable for, which the document sets out as a principle of the new model. None of those is a power to stop a project, and the document does not describe one.

Advice rights depend on whether anyone can check them afterwards, and this is where I would look, because assurance is the least developed part of the reform. The document plans a Monitoring and Evaluation Framework with “a focused set of performance measures” and says nothing about publishing it (Department of Defence, 2026b, p. 35). The Auditor-General has listed the establishment of the agency as a potential topic for 2027-29, which is after it exists (Australian National Audit Office, 2026). Independent scrutiny of defence acquisition has been contracting for a while; I wrote in April about [the discontinuation of the Major Projects Report](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/04/15/reforms-without-scrutiny-concentrating-defence-acquisition-authority-as-oversight-retreats/), and in July about [how little a new agency settles on its own](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/07/30/the-new-agency-is-the-easy-part/). Whether this advice right amounts to anything will depend on whether it is recorded against the project it concerns, and read back to that project three years later.

Which makes the next eight months more consequential than a designate period sounds. Parker inherits a finished design, but the delegations and the working boundary with the capability managers are what the transition year is for, and she negotiates them from inside the department before she runs anything outside it. Those negotiations set the terms on which an additional $117 billion of defence investment, across two national defence strategies out to 2035-36, turns into equipment.

The appointment has been read as a bet on a person: whether someone from mining can deliver military capability. On the published design, what she must deliver is set by whoever confirms the requirement, at a gate she only advises. The delegations are being written now, in the eight months before her appointment comes with an organisation attached.

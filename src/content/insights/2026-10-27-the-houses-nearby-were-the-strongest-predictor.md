The Palisades Fire started on the morning of 7 January 2025 and burnt for most of the month. By the time CAL FIRE recorded containment on the 31st, it had covered 23,448 acres and destroyed 6,845 structures. Twelve civilians died (California Department of Forestry and Fire Protection, 2026). A paper published in *Frontiers in Sustainability* on 7 October this year took 12,081 damage inspections from that fire and asked one narrow question of them. Of everything you could measure about a house before a fire reaches it, which measurement best predicts whether the house survives? Across the inspected residential set, where 5,566 of 9,883 houses were destroyed, the variable that did most of the work was the number of other buildings standing within 100 metres (Farajpoor & Narimani, 2026a).

The effect is a large one. The odds of destruction rose 3.74-fold for every standard deviation of neighbourhood building count, which made it the strongest of the predictors they tested. Vegetation still mattered, though not in the direction a homeowner would expect: higher neighbourhood moisture went with lower odds of destruction, while greenness changed sign depending on what else was included alongside it. The authors are careful about what they have. These are associations drawn from a single wind-driven fire in one city’s housing stock. Building count could be standing in for a suburb’s construction era or its position along the fire’s path instead of acting on its own.

In Australia, household preparation is organised around the lot, and so are the instruments underneath it. When a house is built on bushfire prone land, the construction standard it has to meet is set by its attack level. AS 3959 works that out from four things. One is the fire danger index for the area. The other three are the classification of the vegetation near the site, how far the house sits from that vegetation, and the effective slope of the ground beneath it. Some vegetation is set aside as low threat and needs no separation distance at all. Maintained lawns and cultivated gardens sit in that category. Three of those four inputs turn on the vegetation nearby and the ground beneath it. None is a measure of what else has been built close by.

On the Australian evidence, that is a defensible way to build the rules. Owen Price and David Bowman published an analysis of house loss across the 2019-20 fire season in the *International Journal of Wildland Fire* in August, and the title states their conclusion: the official risk mapping holds up. Of the houses destroyed, 81 per cent stood inside flammable vegetation, 13 per cent in the 100 metre buffer beside it, and 6 per cent further out than that. Loss rates fell away with distance, from 5.4 per cent within 50 metres of vegetation to 1 per cent or less past 200 metres. On the mechanism the Palisades paper points at, they report no obvious evidence of house-to-house transmission, and say the few losses at a distance were mostly in low-density agricultural country (Price & Bowman, 2026).

Two well-evidenced papers from the same year point at different fuels, and the reason is that they are not describing the same kind of fire. The 2019-20 disaster was overwhelmingly bush fire arriving at a vegetation edge, and even the exceptions ran that way. Of the 148 houses lost more than 100 metres out from vegetation, just 2 per cent were in urban settings. Agricultural and rural country accounted for just over half of them. Palisades was a dense suburb that burnt. Price and Bowman also show their own finding weakening as conditions worsen. Among houses outside flammable vegetation, the share of losses beyond 100 metres rose from 29 per cent in mild weather to 64 per cent once the forest fire danger index passed 100. The buffer predicts survival well, and does so least well on the days that produce the most loss.

Which is where the Australian instruments run out of inputs. Nothing in the attack level determination asks how many houses stand within 100 metres of the one being assessed. For a fire that arrives through vegetation, that omission is correct. Adding a density term would only add work to an instrument that already does its job. For a fire where the strongest single signal sits at the scale of the surrounding neighbourhood, the determination has no input that would register it. CSIRO’s account of how buildings ignite treats consequential fires as a major cause of house ignition, and describes a small fire left beside a house growing until it breaks a vulnerable part of the building envelope. So the mechanism is well understood, and the assessment still has nowhere to put it.

In New South Wales there is a window open on precisely this. Planning for Bush Fire Protection is what the Rural Fire Service calls its principal statutory instrument for development on bushfire prone land. The version in force, PBP 2019, was picked up in the planning regulations on 1 March 2020. A draft PBP 2026 went on public exhibition from 6 August to 3 September this year. That period has closed, the Service says the feedback will be considered in preparing the final document, and the exhibited draft cannot be relied on until PBP 2026 is published and recognised under the legislation (NSW Rural Fire Service, 2026). Whether a neighbourhood term belongs in the instrument, and which Australian streets are dense enough for it to change an outcome, is a question that has somewhere to go for the next few months.

It is more a planning matter than a fire agency one, which is part of why it is awkward to place. Separation distances and subdivision layout are decided years before any fire, by consent authorities, in instruments a fire authority advises on without owning. Disclosure and insurance reprice the risk once the boundary is drawn, as [A 4.2% Discount for Telling the Truth](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/07/20/a-4-2-discount-for-telling-the-truth-what-was-bushfire-maps-show-about-disclosure-as-policy/) set out, but that works downstream of the subdivision decision. The household lever is real too, and I have argued in [What Would Make You Go?](https://russellbuzby.com/2026/09/21/what-would-make-you-go/) that what households lack is a named trigger. It is also the only one of the three an owner controls, and on this evidence the smallest of them.

One practical note for anyone about to quote the Palisades paper: the version you pick changes the number. The preprint, posted on 23 August, put the headline figure at 4.12-fold per standard deviation. Six weeks later, the reviewed paper says 3.74. Two of the preprint’s more specific results are missing from the published abstract altogether: an odds ratio for moisture at 100 to 300 metres, and another for greenness at 30 to 100 metres. Its clean statement that predictive information concentrated at the neighbourhood scale became a hedged comparison whose variability between folds overlapped (Farajpoor & Narimani, 2026b). Neither the authors nor the data changed between versions. The reviewed one claims less, and it is the one to cite.

The paper is candid about its own performance too, and reports it twice. Under random cross-validation the gradient-boosting model reached a ROC-AUC of 0.92. When whole square kilometres were held out of training, that fell to 0.75. Plain logistic regression did about as well under the harder test and was better calibrated than the complicated model.

A single extra input would let an assessment ask the question: a count of structures inside a fixed radius, which is what the Palisades study took from OpenStreetMap. For most of Australia that number would change nothing, because the bush is still what arrives first. The few Australian streets where it would matter are the ones no assessment is currently obliged to look at.
